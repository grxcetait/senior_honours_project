A cosmological neutral hydrogen (HI) intensity mapping simulation was generated using the meer21cm package (\cite{Meer21cmMeer21cmDocumentation}), which allows for the independent generation of the various signal components expected in a real HI intensity mapping observation, including the 21\,cm signal, smooth foreground emission, polarised foregrounds, and instrumental noise.

% [1] https://inspirehep.net/literature/3094605#:~:text=Citations%20per%20year&text=The%20MeerKAT%20Large%20Area%20Synoptic,5%E2%80%B2%E2%80%B2.
% [2] https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/469/1/787/3098176
The mock 21\,cm signal was generated using the \texttt{MockSimulation} class, adopting the MeerKAT Large Area Synoptic Survey (MeerKLASS) survey observed with the MeerKAT radio telescope in the L-band (856-1712\,MHz) data [1], covering a smaller frequency range of []. This corresponds to a redshift range of approximately z = 0.39 - 0.46, targeting the post-reionisation epoch. A flat-sky approximation was adopted, and a fixed random seed were used to generate the random fields, producing one realisation of the mock data. For all of the data shown in this paper, 100 realisation were used by inputting 100 fixed random seeds. The HI abundance parameter, which defines the ratio of the neutral hydrogen density to the critical density of the Universe at a given redshift, was set to $\Omega_{HI} = 5 \times 10^{-4}$, consistent with observational constraints at this redshift [ref].

The foreground signal was generated using the \texttt{ForegroundSimulation} class with the Haslam backend [ref], which models diffuse Galactic synchrotron emission by extrapolating the 480\,MHz all-sky Haslam map to the survey frequency range using a power=law spectral index of $\alpha = -2.7$, consistent with the expected spectral behaviour of synchrotron radiation. This simulation was projected onto the same sky pixelisation as the mock HI signal using \texttt{HEALPix} with $N_{side} = 128$.

It is important to note that this simulation represents an idealised portrayal of expected observational data. Instrumental noise was not included in order to isolate the performance of the foreground removal methods. Additionally, in order to prevent the results being subject to cosmic variance and to quantify the statistical uncertainty on the recovered power spectrum, the foreground cleaning procedure will be repeated across 100 independent realisations of the mock data, generated by varying the random seeds used in the simulation. Each realisation produces a different but statistically equivalent realisation of the HI signal, and the spread across realisations reflect the sample variance inherent to a single survey volume. The mean and standard deviation of the power spectrum across all realisations were used to produce the data points and error bars shown in the 1D power spectrum plots. This approach provides a more robust assessment of PCA and GPR performance than a single realisation alone. This also allows the consistency of the foreground removal methods to be evaluated across different signal realisations.

By inputting the cosmological models and survey parameters, a mock 21\,cm intensity mapping signal can be simulated. The mock data uses a fixed seed number to generate the random fields, giving one realisation. This can be projected onto the sky where other signals, such as noise and foregrounds, can be added. It can also be seen that they are different in frequency structure. A plot of the frequency versus temperature of the foreground and HI signal can be seen in \Cref{fig:frequency_vs_temperature}. This shows how the two signals are different in frequency structure and are different magnitudes.

The normalised frequency covariance matrices for the total signal, foreground, and HI signal components are shown in \Cref{fig:tot_fg_hi_covariance}. The foreground covariance is nearly uniform across all frequency pairs, where emission at one frequency is strongly correlated with emission at all other frequencies. This reflects its highly smooth spectral behaviour. In contrast, the HI signal covariance is sharply concentrated along the diagonal. 

Both of these figures highlight the difference in the frequency structures of the HI signal and foregrounds, where the foregrounds are smooth while the HI signal varies rapidly across frequencies. It is clear that the foreground dominates the total signal, being four orders of magnitude larger than the HI signal. It can also be seen that there are frequencies where the foreground and HI signal overlap. This presents the challenge when performing foreground cleaning without removing some of the underlying HI signal.

This fundamental difference in covariance structure, combined with the foreground being approximately four orders of magnitude brighter than the HI signal as shown in \Cref{fig:map_w_wo_foreground} and \Cref{fig:frequency_vs_temperature}, is what both PCA and GPR exploit to achieve foreground separation.
