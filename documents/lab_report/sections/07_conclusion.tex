\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This project investigated two foreground removal methods for 21\,cm intensity mapping, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), with the goal of recovering the HI power spectrum from simulated data. This simulated data was contaminated by foreground emission several orders of magnitude brighter than the cosmological signal and had was smooth in frequency in comparison to the HI signal which varied rapidly in frequency. Both methods were evaluated through their ability to recover the spherically averaged 1D power spectrum and the cylindrical power spectrum.

PCA demonstrated a strong performance with the defined $k$ threshold of $k_{\parallel} = 0.4$\,Mpc$^{-1}$, successfully recovering the HI power spectrum. By removing three principal components, the bulk of the spectrally smooth foreground emission was successfully subtracted, with the bulk of the residual signal lying within the error bars of the true HI signal along the whole $k$ range. The primary limitation was signal loss at low $k_{\perp}$ and low $k_{\parallel}$, which is a consequence of PCA's inability to distinguish between the foreground and HI signal in modes where their spectral structure overlap. Despite this, PCA was a computationally straightforward and effective method for this data, and overall outperformed GPR in recovering the HI signal. 

GPR offered an approach which instead modelled the HI signal and foreground emission as separate Gaussian processes with distinct covariance structures. A spectral index parameter was incorporated into the foreground kernel to better capture the frequency scaling of the synchrotron emission. MCMC sampling was used to estimate the posterior distribution for all hyperparameters. While the HI amplitude and lengthscale ($\sigma_{HI}$, $l_{HI}$) were well recovered and the spectral index posterior $\gamma$ was consistent with the injected true value of -2.7, the foreground hyperparameters ($\sigma_{fg}$, $l_{fg}$) were poorly constrained with broad and asymmetric posteriors. This reflects the degeneracies within the foreground covariance model that the data could not resolve. As a result, the GPR foreground removal was less complete compared to PCA, with residual foreground persisting across the whole k range.

It is important to note that both methods were evaluated on simulated data in the absence of instrumental noise, polarised foreground, radio frequency interference, and other contributions and represents an idealised scenario far from real observational data. The simulated frequency range of 9701.2 - 1023.6\,MHz is also relatively narrow, which directly limits the GPR's ability to constrain the foreground hyperparameters. A wider bandwidth would likely improve the MCMC posterior constraints. Additionally, no beam effects, calibration errors, or radio frequency interference were included in the simulation, all of which would include additional challenges for both methods in a realistic scenario. 

\subsection{Future Work}

There are several natural extensions of this work which would address the limitations identified above. The most immediate improvement would be to apply both methods to simulated data that includes realistic instrumental noise and beam effects, as these are expected to significantly impact the performance of foreground removal. Additionally, testing on a wider frequency range would be beneficial as this would allow for us to figure out whether the GPR hyperparameter constrains improve or if the performance of PCA would maintain its success with an increased frequency bandwidth.

For GPR, more flexible kernel choices or a multi-component foreground model could better capture the complexity of real foreground emission, such as adding polarised foregrounds and a spectral index which varies instead of stays constant. For PCA, study on the optimal number of components $N_{FG}$ and its relation to the frequency range of data would be valuable, as the choice of three components was based on visual inspection of the power spectrum rather than a selection based on a formal criteria.

The comparison between PCA and GPR highlights a general tension in foreground removal methods between simplicity and physical interpretability. PCA purely relies on the statistical structure in the data, while GPR uses physical assumptions directly into the kernel. In the idealised simulated data studied in this paper, PCA's simplicity proved advantageous. However, as observations approach the sensitivity of instruments such as MeerKAT, the ability of GPR to propagate uncertainties and incorporate physical priors may be extremely valuable. Future work in combining both approaches, for example using PCA as an initialisation step for GPR hyperparameter priors, could be a path towards a robust foreground removal for next-generation 21\,cm intensity mapping surveys.
