\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Here, we will present the main results which is the performance of both PCA and GPR as foreground removal methods. First, the results for PCA will be presented, secondly, GPR.

\subsection{Principal Component Analysis}

The performance of the PCA foreground removal was evaluated by comparing the recovered residual signal to the true cosmological signal through the cylindrical and spherically averaged 1D power spectra. First, we will discuss the choice of foreground principal components. Then, the results from the foreground removal which is the 1D and cylindrical power spectrum will be discussed. Finally, we will finish with a review on the overall performance of PCA.

\subsubsection{Foreground Principal Components}

The choice of $N_{FG} = 3$ was decided based on inspection of the eigenvectors and eigenvalues. This choice of $N_{FG}$ components involves a trade-off where too few components leave residual foreground, while too many risk subtracting the genuine HI signal. The first three modes captured the foreground-dominated variance while the remaining modes were consistent with the cosmological signal. 

% [1] https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.12665
The choice of $N_{FG} = 3$ is much lower than what was used in \cite{soaresGaussianProcessRegression2022}, who used $N_{FG} = 6$ and $N_{FG} = 7$. This is likely due to their more complex data, which includes instrumental noise, beam effects, and polarised foregrounds which are absent from this simulation; this analysis only considers the cosmological signal and the foreground. The results suggests that three components is a reasonable choice for this simulation, though a more rigorous approach could be taken which involves cross-validating the choice of $N_{FG}$ against the recovered power spectrum as a function of component number. 

\subsubsection{Cylindrical Power Spectrum}

The cylindrical power spectrum of the true HI power spectrum, the residual signal after PCA cleaning, and their ratio (see \Cref{fig:cylindrical_power_spectrum_None_None}) was used as an assessment of the foreground cleaning. The ratio panel provided the clearest diagnostic of the cleaning performance. 

At high $k_{\parallel}$, the ratio is close to unity and uniformly red, indicating that the recovered signal is in good agreement with the true HI power spectrum in these modes. This is physically expected as high $k_{\parallel}$ modes correspond to small-scale structure along the line of sight that varies rapidly with frequency and is therefore largely orthogonal to the spectrally smooth foreground components removed by PCA. 

% [1] https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/446/4/3545/2891891
% [2] https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1086/668105/pdf#:~:text=This%20work%20was%20originally%20developed%20for%20PCA,underlying%20methodology%20is%20specific%20to%20astronomical%20spectra.
% [3] http://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/478/3/3640/4995230#118057951
% [4] https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21626
However, at low $k_{\parallel}$, the ratio drops below unity, indicating significant signal loss. Here, the 21\,cm signal shares spectral structure with the foregrounds and cannot be cleanly separated by PCA. This inevitably removes some genuine HI signal alongside the foreground components, a known limitation of blind component separation techniques (\cite{delchambreWeightedPrincipalComponent2015a}, \cite{baileyPrincipalComponentAnalysis2012b}). This signal loss at low $k_{\parallel}$ is consistent with findings in \cite{mertensStatistical21cmSignal2018} and \cite{collaborationMeerKLASSLbandDeepfield2025a}, who report the same characteristic suppression of modes at low $k_{\parallel}$ from PCA cleaning. 

Through inspection of the ratio panel, a minimum threshold of $k_\parallel = 0.4$\,Mpc$^{-1}$ was defined and the foreground cleaning was redone excluding modes below these values. Thresholds with values below this were found to still exhibit cosmological signal loss, while thresholds above this value also closely match the true HI signal. The chosen threshold therefore represents the smallest value which maximised foreground cleaning while minimising cosmological signal loss.

\subsubsection{1D Power Spectrum}

\begin{figure}[t!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{outputs/week9/1d_log_power_spectrum_[0, 0.04]_None_both.pdf}

This was run for 100 realisations with the mean used as the datapoints and the standard deviation used as the error bars.}
    \label{fig:1d_log_power_spectrum_0_0.04_None_both}
\end{figure}

The spherically averaged 1D power spectrum with the true HI signal and the residual signal before and after applying the minimum $k$ thresholds can be seen in \Cref{fig:1d_log_power_spectrum_0_0.04_None_both}. Since foreground cleaning was run across 100 independent realisations from 100 fixed seeds, uncertainties were quantified for both the residual signal and the cosmological HI signal, taking into account not only the foreground cleaning but also cosmic variance. Error bars are therefore shown on both the HI signal and the residual.

Prior to applying the minimum thresholds, the residual signal (blue dashed line) follows the true HI signal (black line) at large $k$ but shows a clear deviation at low $k$, consistent with the signal loss identified in the cylindrical power spectrum ratio panel. This deviation arises because PCA removes some genuine cosmological signal alongside the foreground components at these scales.

After applying the minimum thresholds of and $k_\parallel = 0.4$\,Mpc$^{-1}$, the residual signal (red dashed line) shows a stronger agreement with the true HI signal across the full $k$ range, lying within 1$\sigma$ of the HI signal throughout. The error bars are approximately uniform in size at large $k$ but grow larger towards lower $k$, particularly for the threshold residual signal. This is expected and represents the increased variance in the residual signal at the scales most affected by foreground cleaning.

It is also important to note that the residual signal with thresholds does not extend all the way to $k = 0$\,Mpc$^{-1}$, as modes below the threshold are completely excluded from the analysis. While the recovered signal agrees well with the true Hi signal within the defined $k$ ranges, this represents a genuine loss of cosmological information at large scales.

\subsubsection{Overall Performance}

Overall, PCA foreground removal performs well within the defined $k$ thresholds, recovering the HI power spectrum throughout the full $k$ range above $k_{\parallel} = 0.4$\,Mpc$^{-1}$. The primary limitation is the signal loss at low $k_{\parallel}$, which is a consequences of the foreground and HI signal share spectral structure at these scale. 

This analysis represents an idealised scenario where the foreground model assumes a single uniform spectral index across the sky, whereas real foreground have spatially varying spectral indices which would introduce additional mode coupling and make PCA separation more difficult. 

% [1] https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/2041-8205/763/1/L20
% [2] https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/0004-637X/815/1/51
In the case of more complex data more representative of real observations, a transfer function could be considered as an alternative to applying minimum $k$ thresholds (\cite{masuiMEASUREMENT21Cm2013}, \cite{switzerINTERPRETINGUNRESOLVEDINTENSITY2015}). Rather than excluding contaminated modes entirely, a transfer function corrects for signal loss at each $k$ by dividing the cleaned power spectrum by the fraction of the Hi signal retained after cleaning, estimated from the simulations where the true signal is known. 

This analysis would be much more difficult is a more realistic dataset were used or if a more realistic foreground model were used. The foreground signal used in this analysis assumes a single spectral index across the sky, whereas real foregrounds have spatially varying indices which would make PCA separation more difficult.

This preserves cosmological information at low $k_{\parallel}$ that would otherwise be discarded. However, the correction also amplifies any residual foreground contamination or noise present at those modes alongside the HI signal. In this analysis, given that the foreground residuals at low $K_{\parallel}$ are significant, applying a transfer function correction would amplify both the HI signal and the foreground contamination simultaneously. The $k$ threshold approach is therefore the more conservative and ppropriate choice for this dataset, though a transfer function would become the preferred approach as the quality of foreground removal improves.

% [1] https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/2041-8205/763/1/L20
% [2] https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/0004-637X/815/1/51
%In the case of more complex data with components more representative of true observations, another method which could be considered instead of applying minimum $k$ thresholds to mitigates signal loss is to use a transfer function [1], [2]. Instead of excluding the modes which cause the most signal loss, a transfer function is able to keep all $k$ modes but corrects for the signal loss at each one. Instead of completely removing low $k$ modes, it attempts to boost them by the expected suppression factor. However, while this preserves cosmological information, a limitation is that it amplifies noise and foreground residuals while the signal is being boosted. Given the significant foreground residuals present at low $k_{\parallel}$ in this analysis, applying a transfer function correction would amplify both the HI signal and the residual foreground contamination simultaneously, making the k-threshold approach the more conservative and appropriate choice here.

While this simplifies the foreground cleaning, this smaller range allowed for 100 realisation to be run, allowing for the foreground cleaning and cosmic variance to be taken into consideration and error bars to be quantified and plotted on the power spectra, whereas other simulation studies have a larger frequency range but only show a single realisation.

Here, we will discuss the results of foreground removal using GPR. While previous studies including \cite{kernGaussianProcessForeground2020} and \cite{mertensImprovedUpperLimits2020} model the foreground with the standard RBF kernel, this work introduces the spectral-index-modified RBF kernel (\Cref{eq:RBF_sp_indx}) which allows the foreground amplitude to scale as a power law with frequency, physically motivated by the known spectral behaviour of synchrotron emission.

Different combinations of the kernels functions with different initial guesses, bounds, and optimisation algorithms were tested and their LMLs compared to find the one which best fit the data. The exponential function (\Cref{eq:exp}) was found to fit the 21\,cm signal the best in all cases. Additionally, the spectral index modified RBF kernel (\Cref{eq:RBF_sp_indx}) performed the best in comparison to the normal RBF kernel (\Cref{eq:RBF}), as expected as it better models the smooth foregrounds (see \Cref{fig:rbf_kernel_w_sp_indx_vs_foreground_covariance}). 

First, the hyperparameter estimations from MCMC sampling will be discussed, along with an assessment of the chain convergence. Then, the results from the foreground removal, which is the 1D and cylindrical power spectrum, will be presented, followed by an evaluation of the overall GPR performance.

The posterior distributions of the GPR hyperparameters were explored using MCMC sampling, with the resulting corner plot shown in \Cref{fig:final_corner_plot_values}. The model includes five hyperparameters: the amplitude and the length scale of the HI signal kernel and the foreground kernel ($\sigma_{HI}$, $\sigma_{fg}$, ${l_{HI}}$, ${l_{fg}}$), and the foreground spectral index ($\gamma$). Together, these parameters control how the GPR model separates the spectrally smooth foreground from the HI signal which varies rapidly with frequency. A summary of the recovered hyperparameter values can be seen in \Cref{tab:hyperparameters}. 

The recovered HI amplitude $\sigma_{HI}$ is the most tightly constrained of all five hyperparameters. This tight constraint is consistent across all kernel combinations tested prior to MCMC sampling, suggesting the GPR model reliably identifies the amplitude of the HI fluctuations even in the presence of dominant foreground contamination. The narrow, symmetric posterior for $\sigma_{HI}$ visible in \Cref{fig:final_corner_plot_values} confirms this is the best-determined parameter in the model.

The recovered HI lengthscale $l_{HI}$ is moderately constrained, though the posterior has a tail towards larger values. Since $l_{HI}$ controls the frequency correlation of the HI kernel, a smaller value produces more rapidly varying functions, which is expected from the HI signal as seen in \Cref{fig:exp_kernel_lengthscales}. The recovered value is therefore physically reasonable, though the tail tending towards larger values reflects some degeneracy with $\sigma_{HI}$, as seen in the elongated diagonal contour between these two parameters in \Cref{fig:final_corner_plot_values}, which is a contrast to the rounder, less correlated contours of the other parameter pairs.

The foreground amplitude $\sigma_{fg}$ is considerably less well constrained with a broad and asymmetric posterior. This reflects a degeneracy in the foreground model where the observed total covariance can be reported by different combinations of amplitude and lengthscale, making it difficult to constrain these parameters independently. 

The foreground lengthscale $l_{fg}$ has a particularly large and asymmetric uncertainty. The posterior rises steeply and has a long tail towards higher values, suggesting the data is consistent with a wide range of $l_{fg}$. $l_{fg}$ consistently approached the upper bound of the prior across all optimisation attempts regardless of initial guesses or bound choices. Physically, this large $l_{fg}$ corresponds to foreground emissions that is highly correlated in frequency and, therefore, very smooth. This is consistent with what we expect with synchrotron-dominated foregrounds. However, the poor constraint indicates that the frequency range used may be insufficient to fully characterise $l_{fg}$. A wider bandwidth may be beneficial to characterise $l_{fg}$ reliably.

Finally, the foreground spectral index $\gamma$ is recovered with a broad posterior. The injected true value of -2.7 lies within the 1$\sigma$ credible interval, confirming the model is recovering physically consistent spectral behaviour despite the large uncertainty. The broad posterior is consistent with the difficulty of constraining the spectral shape over a narrow frequency range. The diffuse joint posterior between $l_{fg}$ and $\gamma$ in \Cref{fig:final_corner_plot_values} confirms a further degeneracy where a larger spectral index combined with a longer length scale can produce a similar foreground covariance structure to a smaller spectral index with a shorter length scale. This degeneracy makes these parameters difficult to constrain independently.

The MCMC trace plots for all five hyperparameters are shown in \Cref{fig:mcmc_chain_with_sp_indx_fg_no_noise}. Trace plots verify that the chains have converged and are efficiently exploring the parameter space. Each coloured line represents an individual walker's trajectory through parameter space over 10,000 steps. For a well-converged sampler, the traces should form dense stationary bands with no systematic upward or downward drift.

All five hyperparameters show good mixing across all walkers, with no systematic drift evident in any chain. The mean acceptance fraction was approximately 0.46 for both the burn-in and sampling phase, indicating an optimal balance between step size and posterior exploration. $\sigma_{HI}$, $l_{HI}$ and $\gamma$ are recovered with reasonably well-defined posteriors, while $\sigma_{fg}$ and $l_{fg}$ remain poorly constrained due to the degeneracy between the foreground amplitude and length scale. Most importantly, no systematic drift is present, confirming that the large spread reflects the genuine posterior landscape rather than a failure of the sampler to converge. The fitted kernels alongside the true covariance signals are shown in \Cref{fig:kernel_comparison_mcmc_with_sp_indx_no_noise}.

The cylindrical power spectrum provides additional information on the foreground removal by separating modes along the line of sight ($k_{\parallel}$) from transverse modes ($k_{\perp}$). \Cref{fig:gpr_cylindrical_scipy_with_no_noise_and_sp_indx} shows the true HI power spectrum, the GPR residual signal, and their ratio. 

The ratio panel reveals that across the majority of $k$ modes, the ratio is close to unity, indicating a mostly successful foreground removal. However, at low $k_{\perp}$ across a range of $k_{\parallel}$ values, the ratio exceeds unity, indicating residual foreground contamination at large transverse spatial scales. This under-cleaning is consistent with the fitted foreground kernel underestimating the true foreground covariance amplitude, leaving excess foreground power at the large-scale modes where the foreground contribution is strongest. Conversely, at low $k_{\parallel}$ and high $k_{\perp}$, the ratio falls below unity, indicating mild signal loss at small line-of-sight scale combined with large transverse scales. This mixed behaviour reflected the poorly constrained foreground length scale, which limited the foreground contribution model across all $k$ modes. This is in contrast to the PCA cylindrical power spectrum (\Cref{fig:cylindrical_power_spectrum_None_None}), where the ratio is close to unity across almost the entire panel above the applied $k_{\parallel}$ threshold.

\subsubsection{1D Power Spectrum}

The spherically averaged log 1D power spectrum before and after GPR foreground removal is shown in \Cref{fig:final_power_spectrum_1}. The black curve represents the true HI power spectrum whereas the red dashed curve shows the recovered power spectrum after GPR cleaning, with error bars derived from 1,000 posterior samples drawn from the MCMC chain.

At high $k$ ($k \gtrsim 0.4$\,Mpc$^{-1}$), the residual power spectrum lies close to the true HI signal, with the error bars remaining narrow, indicating that the foreground contribution is small at these scales and the GPR cleaning is effective. However, at low k ($k \lesssim 0.4$\,Mpc$^{-1}$), the residual signal deviates significantly from the true HI signal, rising approximately 3-4 orders of magnitude above the true HI signal. This large excess is attributable to residual foreground power that the GPR cleaning was unable to subtract. This is a direct consequence of the underestimated foreground amplitude seen in \Cref{fig:kernel_comparison_mcmc_with_sp_indx_no_noise}. This is a constrast to the PCA result (\Cref{fig:1d_log_power_spectrum_0_0.04_None_both}), where the residual signal lies within the 1$\sigma$ error bars of the true HI signal across the full $k$ range after the applied minimum thresholds.

It is important to note the error bars on the residual signal represent the 1$\sigma$ credible interval derived from 1,000 parameter sets drawn at random from the MCMC posterior chain. Each draw produces a distinct set of kernel hyperparameters, which are used to clean the same fixed map realisation. The 16th and 84th percentiles across these 1,000 cleaned power spectra are used as the error bars. These error bars are notably small at large $k$, where the foreground contribution is negligible. At low $k$, the error bars grow larger, reflecting the increased sensitivity of the foreground removal to the poorly constrained foreground parameters at these scales.

\subsubsection{Overall Performance}

The GPR foreground removal demonstrates partial but incomplete recovery of the HI power spectrum. The method successfully removed the bulk of the foreground contamination at high $k$ ($k \gtrsim 0.4$\,Mpc$^{-1}$), where the residual signal closely follows the true HI power spectrum. However, significant foreground residuals persist at low $k$, where the recovered power spectrum exceeds the true HI signal by 3-4 orders of magnitude. This incomplete removal is primarily attributed to the degeneracy between the foreground amplitude $\sigma_{fg}$ and length scale $l_{fg}$, which resulted in the fitted foreground kernel underestimating the true foreground covariance amplitude by approximately a factor of two. Despite this, the HI amplitude $\sigma_{HI}$ is well recovered and spectral index $\gamma$ is consistent with the injected value of -2.7. 

The uncertainty quantification presented here reflects only hyperparameter uncertainty from a single sky realisation. Running MCMC across 100 independent realisations, as done for PCA, would additionally cpature cosmic variance and provide a fairer comparison for the uncertainty estimate between the two methods.

Additionally, a larger frequency range may be beneficial to improve on the hyperparameter constraints if there is more frequency information to learn from since GPR uses frequency data. While \cite{hothiComparingForegroundRemoval2021} found that GPR performed worse with a larger frequency range, \cite{soaresGaussianProcessRegression2022} found that GPR performed better with a smaller and lower frequency range, and performed worse with smaller and higher frequency range. This worse performance with the smaller and higher frequency range was due to their foreground amplitude $\sigma_{fg}$.
