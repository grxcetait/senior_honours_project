In 1929, Edwin Hubble confirmed the Universe was expanding through observations of measurements of the redshift of spiral galaxies [1]. Scientists previously thought that the Universe's expansion would have likely been slowed down by gravity. This thought was backed up by Einstein's theory of general relativity. However, through the observations of a distant type Ia supernovae appearing further and fainter than expected led to scientists concluding that the universe must be accelerating [2]. This discovery significantly changed our understanding of the Universe. Driven by these results, the scientific community studied several methods, such as the Cosmic Microwave Background, Supernovae Type Ia, and Baryon Acoustic Oscillations, to better understand this acceleration [3].

Since this breakthrough, as instrumental technology advanced, the volume and prevision of cosmological data increased significantly and led to high percent precision in cosmological parameters. While the majority of observations agree with the predictions of the standard cosmological model, there are discrepancies between these methods, known as cosmological probes [4]. The most prominent being the discrepancy between the Hubble constant $H_0$ determined from the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) [5] not being in agreement with the one determined from type Ia supernovae and Cepheids [6]. Although this discrepancy has been discussed at length in literature, there is no agreement in the scientific community on the source [7] [8] [9]. Due to the increased precision in the current cosmological probes becoming close to reaching its maximum, it's prudent to look for new and independent cosmological probes that could either confirm or deny the discrepancies found [10].

Probing the large scale structure (LSS) of the Universe is crucial in providing information about the very early Universe, the distribution of dark matter, galaxy formation, and measuring the Universe's acceleration [11]. Traditionally, this involves measuring the redshift and angular position of galaxies individually, then creating a catalogue and 3D map in the optical or near-infrared [12]. This method led to constraints on dark energy, gravity, and the initial conditions of the Universe. However, this method required the often difficult detection of individual galaxies which becomes expensive. An alternative and novel probe for the large-scale structure (LSS) of the Universe is Neutral Hydrogen (HI) Intensity Mapping [13] which does not require the often difficult and expensive detection of individual galaxies but rather the cumulative emission of HI flux in the sky [14]. Hence, it can map large areas of the sky very quickly.

Following the epoch of reionization, majority of HI exists mainly inside of self shielding galaxies. This makes HI an excellent tool for probing how matter is distributed throughout the Universe. The 21cm line from the spin flip transition is dominant spectral line in astronomical observations at frequencies less than 1420MHz [14]. By observing this redshifted line using radio telescopes, and due to the fact it is an isolated transition, a 3D map of LSS in the Universe can be obtained. This is advantageous relative to the 2D surface probed by the CMB [15]. This is because the 21cm line provides the radial coordinate along the line-of-sight which. This signal can also be observed from the dark ages, to the epoch or reionization, to the current epoch [16] [17], meaning they extend to a high enough redshift where most of the co-moving volume of the Universe resides. Therefore, HI is a good probe for the matter distribution in the Universe.
